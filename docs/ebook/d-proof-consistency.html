Appendix D

Proof of Consistency

Chapter 14 states that the core system is consistent: no closed term has type Π(P:Sort 0). P\tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}Π(P:Sort0).P (Theorem 14.5). This appendix gives the full proof, including the standard facts about typing and conversion that the argument uses. It concerns the core system: the rules of Figure 3.1 with the universes of Chapter 13, without the constants of Part II.

The idea

The proof combines a deep theorem with an elementary argument. The deep theorem is strong normalization: every well-typed term reduces to a normal form. Together with subject reduction, it means that if any closed term had type Π(P:Sort 0). P\tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}Π(P:Sort0).P, a normal closed term would too. The elementary argument is a case analysis showing that no normal closed term can have that type.

The case analysis runs in two rounds. First, the normal form must be a λ\lambdaλ, because nothing else closed and normal can have a Π\PiΠ type. Second, its body must be a term of type PPP in the context P:Sort 0P : \Sort{0}P:Sort0, and no normal term can be, because the only variable in scope has type Sort 0\Sort{0}Sort0, which is neither PPP nor a function type.

To make the case analysis rigorous, we need to know what the type of a term of each shape looks like (the generation lemma), and that terms of different shapes are never convertible (a consequence of confluence). Those facts are collected first.

Facts about the type system

Besides subject reduction and strong normalization from Chapter 14, the proof uses the following standard properties of Pure Type Systems [Barendregt 1992]. Subject reduction extends from one step to any number of steps by induction on the number of steps.

Lemma D.1 (Standard properties).

(a) Generation. If Γ⊢t:T\Gamma \vdash t : TΓ⊢t:T, then, depending on the shape of ttt:

  • if t=xt = xt=x, then (x:A)∈Γ(x : A) \in \Gamma(x:A)∈Γ and T≡AT \conv AT≡A for some AAA;
  • if t=Sort ut = \Sort{u}t=Sortu, then T≡Sort (u+1)T \conv \Sort{(u+1)}T≡Sort(u+1);
  • if t=Π(x:A). Bt = \tPi{x}{A}{B}t=Π(x:A).B, then T≡Sort wT \conv \Sort{w}T≡Sortw for some www;
  • if t=λ(x:A). st = \tLam{x}{A}{s}t=λ(x:A).s, then Γ,x:A⊢s:B\Gamma, x : A \vdash s : BΓ,x:A⊢s:B and T≡Π(x:A). BT \conv \tPi{x}{A}{B}T≡Π(x:A).B for some BBB;
  • if t=f  at = f\;at=fa, then Γ⊢f:Π(x:A). B\Gamma \vdash f : \tPi{x}{A}{B}Γ⊢f:Π(x:A).B and Γ⊢a:A\Gamma \vdash a : AΓ⊢a:A for some xxx, AAA, and BBB.

(b) Confluence. If s≡ts \conv ts≡t, then sss and ttt reduce to a common term. Consequently: Π(x:A). B≡Π(y:A′). B′\tPi{x}{A}{B} \conv \tPi{y}{A'}{B'}Π(x:A).B≡Π(y:A′).B′ implies A≡A′A \conv A'A≡A′ and B[x↦z]≡B′[y↦z]\subst{B}{x}{z} \conv \subst{B'}{y}{z}B[x↦z]≡B′[y↦z] for a fresh zzz; and no two of a variable, a universe, and a Π\PiΠ are convertible.

(c) Substitution. If s≡ts \conv ts≡t, then s[z↦a]≡t[z↦a]\subst{s}{z}{a} \conv \subst{t}{z}{a}s[z↦a]≡t[z↦a].

Generation is the typing rules read backwards: it says that the only way to type a term of a given shape is the rule for that shape, possibly followed by uses of T-Conv. That is why each case says "T≡…T \conv \ldotsT≡…" rather than "T=…T = \ldotsT=…".

The consequences in (b) hold because a variable and a universe do not reduce at all, a Π\PiΠ reduces only to a Π\PiΠ (reduction happens inside its annotation or body, never at its root), and two terms with a common reduct must therefore have the same outermost shape.

Shapes of normal terms

Recall from Chapter 12 that every term is a spine h  a1⋯anh\;a_1 \cdots a_nha1​⋯an​ whose head hhh is not an application. The next lemma classifies normal terms by their head. Its role is to leave only four possibilities for the normal form in the main proof, each of which can then be ruled out.

Lemma D.2 (Normal forms). Let nnn be a normal term with Γ⊢n:T\Gamma \vdash n : TΓ⊢n:T. Then nnn is a universe, a Π\PiΠ, a λ\lambdaλ, or a neutral term x  a1⋯akx\;a_1 \cdots a_kxa1​⋯ak​ with k≥0k \ge 0k≥0 whose head xxx is a variable in dom(Γ)\dom(\Gamma)dom(Γ). And if k≥1k \ge 1k≥1, then Γ⊢x:C\Gamma \vdash x : CΓ⊢x:C for some CCC that is convertible to a Π\PiΠ.

Write n=h  a1⋯akn = h\;a_1 \cdots a_kn=ha1​⋯ak​ with hhh not an application.

If k=0k = 0k=0, then n=hn = hn=h is a universe, a Π\PiΠ, a λ\lambdaλ, or a variable, since those are the only shapes other than application. A variable is in dom(Γ)\dom(\Gamma)dom(Γ) by generation.

Let k≥1k \ge 1k≥1. Applying the generation lemma for application kkk times, from the outside in, shows that every prefix h  a1⋯aih\;a_1 \cdots a_iha1​⋯ai​ is typable, and in particular, from the innermost application h  a1h\;a_1ha1​, that Γ⊢h:Π(y:A). B\Gamma \vdash h : \tPi{y}{A}{B}Γ⊢h:Π(y:A).B for some yyy, AAA, and BBB. Now consider the shape of hhh. It is not a λ\lambdaλ, since h  a1h\;a_1ha1​ would then be a β\betaβ-redex inside the normal term nnn. It is not a universe or a Π\PiΠ either: by generation, its type would be convertible to a universe, and by confluence a universe is not convertible to Π(y:A). B\tPi{y}{A}{B}Π(y:A).B. So hhh is a variable xxx, which is in dom(Γ)\dom(\Gamma)dom(Γ) by generation, and Γ⊢x:Π(y:A). B\Gamma \vdash x : \tPi{y}{A}{B}Γ⊢x:Π(y:A).B, which is the required CCC.

Proof of Theorem 14.5

Theorem 14.5 (Consistency). There is no closed term ttt with ⋅⊢t:Π(P:Sort 0). P\cdot \vdash t : \tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}⋅⊢t:Π(P:Sort0).P.

Suppose that ⋅⊢t:Π(P:Sort 0). P\cdot \vdash t : \tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}⋅⊢t:Π(P:Sort0).P. By strong normalization, ttt reduces to a normal term nnn, and by subject reduction, ⋅⊢n:Π(P:Sort 0). P\cdot \vdash n : \tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}⋅⊢n:Π(P:Sort0).P. We show that no such nnn exists, using Lemma D.2 twice.

The normal form is a λ\lambdaλ. By Lemma D.2, nnn is a universe, a Π\PiΠ, a λ\lambdaλ, or a neutral term.

  • It is not neutral, because its head would be a variable in the empty context, and there is none.
  • It is not a universe or a Π\PiΠ, because generation would make Π(P:Sort 0). P\tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}Π(P:Sort0).P convertible to a universe, which confluence rules out.

So n=λ(x:A). sn = \tLam{x}{A}{s}n=λ(x:A).s. By generation, x:A⊢s:Bx : A \vdash s : Bx:A⊢s:B and Π(P:Sort 0). P≡Π(x:A). B\tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P} \conv \tPi{x}{A}{B}Π(P:Sort0).P≡Π(x:A).B for some BBB. By confluence, A≡Sort 0A \conv \Sort{0}A≡Sort0 and P[P↦z]≡B[x↦z]\subst{P}{P}{z} \conv \subst{B}{x}{z}P[P↦z]≡B[x↦z] for a fresh zzz, that is, z≡B[x↦z]z \conv \subst{B}{x}{z}z≡B[x↦z]. Substituting xxx for zzz on both sides, which preserves convertibility by part (c) of Lemma D.1, gives x≡Bx \conv Bx≡B. The body sss is normal, because it is a subterm of the normal term nnn. In short: x:A⊢s:Bx : A \vdash s : Bx:A⊢s:B with A≡Sort 0A \conv \Sort{0}A≡Sort0 and B≡xB \conv xB≡x.

The body cannot exist. Let Γ=x:A\Gamma = x : AΓ=x:A. By Lemma D.2, sss is a universe, a Π\PiΠ, a λ\lambdaλ, or a neutral term.

  • If sss is a universe or a Π\PiΠ, generation makes BBB convertible to a universe; if sss is a λ\lambdaλ, it makes BBB convertible to a Π\PiΠ. But B≡xB \conv xB≡x, and by confluence a variable is convertible to neither.
  • If sss is neutral, its head is a variable in dom(Γ)={x}\dom(\Gamma) = \{x\}dom(Γ)={x}, so s=x  a1⋯aks = x\;a_1 \cdots a_ks=xa1​⋯ak​. If k=0k = 0k=0, then s=xs = xs=x, and generation gives B≡AB \conv AB≡A. Then x≡B≡A≡Sort 0x \conv B \conv A \conv \Sort{0}x≡B≡A≡Sort0, but a variable is not convertible to a universe. If k≥1k \ge 1k≥1, Lemma D.2 gives Γ⊢x:C\Gamma \vdash x : CΓ⊢x:C with CCC convertible to a Π\PiΠ, and generation for the variable xxx gives C≡A≡Sort 0C \conv A \conv \Sort{0}C≡A≡Sort0. Then a Π\PiΠ is convertible to a universe, which confluence rules out.

Every case leads to a contradiction, so no closed term has type Π(P:Sort 0). P\tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}Π(P:Sort0).P.

In words: the only variable available inside the λ\lambdaλ is xxx, whose type is Sort 0\Sort{0}Sort0. It can neither be returned, since Sort 0\Sort{0}Sort0 is not xxx, nor be applied to anything, since Sort 0\Sort{0}Sort0 is not a function type. Every other normal term has a universe or a Π\PiΠ as its type, and xxx is neither. The argument relies on strong normalization to reduce the question to normal terms; that step is where all the difficulty lies.

The second half of the proof, the analysis of normal terms, does not use strong normalization and does not depend on the universe structure. It carries over unchanged to a system with Type:Type\mathsf{Type} : \mathsf{Type}Type:Type. That is how Chapter 13 concludes that Hurkens' term (Appendix C), which has type Π(P:Type). P\tPi{P}{\mathsf{Type}}{P}Π(P:Type).P in that system, cannot have a normal form: if it did, the analysis would rule it out. So in the inconsistent system it is strong normalization that fails, not generation or confluence.

With the inductive types of Part II, normal forms also include stuck recursor applications, such as Nat.recu  M  z  s  n\NatRec{u}\;M\;z\;s\;nNat.recu​Mzsn with nnn a variable, and the short case analysis above no longer suffices on its own. Consistency of the Calculus of Inductive Constructions, of which the extended system is a fragment, follows from strong normalization by a more careful analysis of normal forms [Werner 1994].

What to remember

  • Consistency is proved by combining strong normalization and subject reduction (to pass to a normal form) with a case analysis on the shape of normal terms.
  • The case analysis uses two standard facts: generation (the typing rules read backwards) and confluence (terms of different outermost shapes are never convertible).
  • A normal closed term of type Π(P:Sort 0). P\tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}Π(P:Sort0).P would have to be a λ\lambdaλ whose body has type xxx in the context x:Sort 0x : \Sort{0}x:Sort0, and no normal term does.
  • The hard part is strong normalization; the shape analysis alone also applies to Type:Type\mathsf{Type} : \mathsf{Type}Type:Type, and shows that Hurkens' term has no normal form.
