Chapter 4

The Typing Rules as a Logic

Chapter 3 presented eight typing rules as rules of logic. To make that claim precise, this chapter chooses a particular logic, translates its formulas into types, and shows that every proof in the logic becomes a term accepted by the kernel. It then explains why the kernel also needs consistency to be trustworthy.

A logic to compare against

The logic has formulas built from proposition variables P,Q,…P, Q, \ldotsP,Q,… by implication and by quantification over propositions:

φ,ψ  ::=  P  ∣  φ→ψ  ∣  ∀P. φ\varphi, \psi \;::=\; P \;\mid\; \varphi \to \psi \;\mid\; \forall P.\,\varphiφ,ψ::=P∣φ→ψ∣∀P.φ

This is second-order propositional logic: "second-order" because ∀\forall∀ ranges over propositions rather than over individuals. It is small, but far from trivial. Conjunction, disjunction, negation, and falsity can all be defined inside it, for example ⊥=∀P. P\bot = \forall P.\,P⊥=∀P.P and φ∧ψ=∀R. (φ→ψ→R)→R\varphi \wedge \psi = \forall R.\,(\varphi \to \psi \to R) \to Rφ∧ψ=∀R.(φ→ψ→R)→R.

Proofs in this logic are natural-deduction derivations of judgments Δ⊢φ\Delta \vdash \varphiΔ⊢φ, where Δ\DeltaΔ is a list of hypotheses. The rules are the ones in the left column of Figure 4.1 below: use a hypothesis, introduce and eliminate implication, introduce and eliminate the quantifier.

Translating formulas into types

Each formula translates to a type, written φ∗\varphi^\astφ∗:

P∗=P,(φ→ψ)∗=φ∗→ψ∗,(∀P. φ)∗=Π(P:Sort 0). φ∗.P^\ast = P, \qquad (\varphi \to \psi)^\ast = \varphi^\ast \to \psi^\ast, \qquad (\forall P.\,\varphi)^\ast = \tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{\varphi^\ast}.P∗=P,(φ→ψ)∗=φ∗→ψ∗,(∀P.φ)∗=Π(P:Sort0).φ∗.

A proposition variable becomes a type variable, implication becomes the function type, and quantification over propositions becomes a Π\PiΠ-type over Sort 0\Sort{0}Sort0.

A list of hypotheses translates to a context. Besides one assumption hi:φi∗h_i : \varphi_i^\asthi​:φi∗​ per hypothesis, the context must declare the proposition variables that occur anywhere in the judgment:

(φ1,…,φn)∗  =  P1:Sort 0,…,Pk:Sort 0,  h1:φ1∗,…,hn:φn∗,(\varphi_1, \ldots, \varphi_n)^\ast \;=\; P_1 : \Sort{0}, \ldots, P_k : \Sort{0},\; h_1 : \varphi_1^\ast, \ldots, h_n : \varphi_n^\ast,(φ1​,…,φn​)∗=P1​:Sort0,…,Pk​:Sort0,h1​:φ1∗​,…,hn​:φn∗​,

where P1,…,PkP_1, \ldots, P_kP1​,…,Pk​ are the proposition variables free in Δ\DeltaΔ or in the conclusion φ\varphiφ of the judgment Δ⊢φ\Delta \vdash \varphiΔ⊢φ being translated, and h1,…,hnh_1, \ldots, h_nh1​,…,hn​ are fresh names. The conclusion must be included because a variable may occur only there, as PPP does in ⋅⊢P→P\cdot \vdash P \to P⋅⊢P→P.

Proofs are terms

Theorem 4.1 (Proofs are terms). If Δ⊢φ\Delta \vdash \varphiΔ⊢φ is provable in the logic, then Δ∗⊢t:φ∗\Delta^\ast \vdash t : \varphi^\astΔ∗⊢t:φ∗ for some term ttt.

Figure 4.1 turns each step of a logical proof into a step of a typing derivation, so every proof becomes a term by induction on the proof. Appendix A states the theorem with the scoping of proposition variables made explicit and gives the full proof.

The theorem says that the type checker can check every proof of the logic. For the kernel to be trustworthy, we also need the opposite guarantee: it must not accept a term for a proposition that the logic refutes.

Consistency

Define ⊥=∀P. P\bot = \forall P.\,P⊥=∀P.P, "everything holds". No proof of ⊥\bot⊥ exists in the logic: if each proposition variable is read as true or false, every rule preserves truth under every reading, but ∀P. P\forall P.\,P∀P.P is false under the reading that makes some PPP false.

The type theory should agree. That is, no closed term should have the type

⊥∗  =  Π(P:Sort 0). P.\bot^\ast \;=\; \tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}.⊥∗=Π(P:Sort0).P.

A closed term ttt of this type would be a disaster: for every proposition C:Sort 0C : \Sort{0}C:Sort0, the application t  Ct\;CtC would have type CCC, so every proposition would be provable and the kernel would be worthless.

Definition 4.2 (Consistency). A type system is consistent if no closed term has type Π(P:Sort 0). P\tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}Π(P:Sort0).P.

Consistency is the property that every design decision in this book must preserve. In particular, a design for universes is acceptable only if the system remains consistent; Chapter 13 shows a natural-looking design that fails this test, and Chapter 14 states consistency for our system as Theorem 14.5.

One might hope for the full converse of Theorem 4.1: every term of type φ∗\varphi^\astφ∗ comes from a proof of φ\varphiφ. That stronger property is called conservativity, and it is harder to establish here, because Sort 0\Sort{0}Sort0 also contains types that are not translations of formulas, such as Π(F:Sort 0→Sort 0). F  P→F  P\tPi{F}{\Sort{0} \to \Sort{0}}{F\;P \to F\;P}Π(F:Sort0→Sort0).FP→FP, and a term may pass through them on its way to φ∗\varphi^\astφ∗. We do not need conservativity. Without consistency the kernel's "yes" would mean nothing; with it, the "yes" is enough.

Why single out Π(P:Sort 0). P\tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}Π(P:Sort0).P rather than, say, 0=10 = 10=1? Because it is the universal counterfeit: any proof of it can be converted into a proof of anything else by a single application. If some other absurd statement had a proof, that proof could be used to build a proof of Π(P:Sort 0). P\tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}Π(P:Sort0).P as well, so ruling out this one type rules out all of them.

Exercises

Translate the formula ∀P. ∀Q. (P→Q)→P→Q\forall P.\,\forall Q.\,(P \to Q) \to P \to Q∀P.∀Q.(P→Q)→P→Q to a type, and write the proof term that Figure 4.1 produces for the natural-deduction proof "assume P→QP \to QP→Q and PPP; apply the first to the second".

What to remember

  • Second-order propositional logic translates into the type theory: →\to→ becomes the function type, ∀P\forall P∀P becomes Π(P:Sort 0)\Pi(P : \Sort{0})Π(P:Sort0), hypotheses become context entries.
  • Theorem 4.1: every proof in the logic becomes a well-typed term. The translation is rule by rule (Figure 4.1).
  • The kernel is trustworthy only if it is consistent: no closed term of type Π(P:Sort 0). P\tPi{P}{\Sort{0}}{P}Π(P:Sort0).P.
  • Impredicativity, via imax\imaximax, is what lets a universal statement be instantiated by any formula, including itself.
